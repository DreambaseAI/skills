# Marks: What's Free, What's Restricted, and How to Invent One

Conformity marks are the most recognisable part of the vocabulary and the only part that carries legal risk. This file makes the distinction usable.

**This is design guidance assembled from primary legal sources, not legal advice.** For anything that ships as a physical product, on packaging, or in advertising, the answer is always "get real advice" — and the skill's job is to raise that before the work is built, not after.

## The four tiers

The key insight: **"free artwork" and "free to apply" are different questions.**

| Tier | Examples | Artwork | Applying it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Legal conformity marks** | CE, UKCA, FCC logo, RCM, PSE, KC, CCC, WEEE bin | **Freely distributed** — the European Commission hands out CE files in AI/EPS/PNG, gov.uk distributes UKCA templates, the FCC logo is a figure printed in 47 CFR §2.1074 and a US Government work | **Restricted.** Affixing is limited to products the legislation covers, and **lookalikes are banned** |
| **(b) Private certification marks** | UL, ETL, CSA, TÜV (several separate companies), VDE, ENEC | Registered trademarks / certification marks, issued only under signed agreement | **No safe decorative reproduction at all** |
| **(c) Informational symbols** | IEC 60417, ISO 7000 | Copyrighted database, licensed by subscription | Free of certification claims — these are the **safe raw material for texture** |
| **(d) Genuinely free** | AIGA/DOT Symbol Signs, Unicode, US Government works | Public domain — AIGA's own words: "These copyright-free symbols…" | Free |

**Tier (c) is where most of the visual register actually lives.** AC/DC symbols, the Class II double square, the indoor-use house, the hazard triangle, the crossed-out bin, the Mobius loop — these carry the whole look of a rating plate while making no certification claim about anything.

## What a designer may and may not do

**May:**

- Draw, publish, and distribute the **CE** and **UKCA** marks as artwork — the issuing authorities distribute the files themselves.
- Reproduce the **FCC logo**, a US Government work printed in the CFR.
- Use the **AIGA/DOT symbol signs** and **Unicode** symbols freely.
- Apply **IEC 60417 / ISO 7000** symbols to equipment, having paid for database access.
- **Depict real marks editorially** — explaining, illustrating, reporting on them. Non-source-designating expressive use remains defensible after *Jack Daniel's v. VIP* (2023), which confined the doctrine to uses where a mark performs "some other expressive function" rather than designating source.
- **Recolour or hollow the CE mark** — the Blue Guide expressly permits different forms including colour and solid/hollow, provided proportions and legibility hold.

**May not:**

- Affix CE to a product not covered by legislation requiring it. The Commission's phrasing is blunt: "It is forbidden to affix CE marking to other products."
- Distort CE/UKCA proportions on a product, or go below the **5 mm minimum height**.
- Affix any marking "likely to mislead third parties regarding the meaning **or form**" of CE/UKCA. **This clause is the important one** — see below.
- Reproduce **UL, ETL, CSA, TÜV, VDE, ENEC** or any private certification mark without a signed agreement. CSA: "The marks must never be altered in any manner." Intertek: "You may enlarge or reduce the Mark proportionally, but it cannot be changed in any other way."
- Show a certification mark without its mandated identifier — ENEC lists this explicitly as misuse.
- Display a trust or quality mark **in any commercial context** without authorisation.
- Traffic in **labels or stickers** bearing a counterfeit mark — this reaches the artwork itself, not just marked goods.

### The lookalike clause closes the obvious escape hatch

The instinct when told "don't use CE" is to draw something CE-shaped but different. **That is precisely what the law targets.** Reg. (EC) 765/2008 Art. 30(5) — and the identical UKCA provision — prohibit markings likely to mislead as to the meaning **or form** of the mark.

So there are two safe positions and one dangerous one in the middle:

- ✅ Show the **genuine mark**, correctly proportioned, as an illustration of the real thing (editorial).
- ✅ Invent something **clearly distinct** — different monogram, different construction.
- ❌ A distorted near-CE. This is the worst option and the one most designers reach for first.

The same logic applies more sharply to tier (b): US law reaches marks "substantially indistinguishable from" a registered mark, which means **a deliberately-almost-UL logo on a product is more exposed than a wholly invented one, not less.**

## The three regimes that bite

They escalate, and they cover different things:

1. **Product** — EU/UK conformity-marking offences (penalties may include criminal sanctions), China's CCC regime, Japan's DENAN.
2. **Marketing — the trap designers miss.** The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, Annex I point 2, makes displaying a trust mark, quality mark, or equivalent **without the necessary authorisation unfair in all circumstances** — no confusion test, no deception required. A landing page or an ad can be an offence with nobody misled. The US analogue is FTC 16 CFR §260.6.
3. **Criminal** — trafficking in counterfeit marks reaches **labels and stickers alone**, with substantial penalties, escalating where injury results.

Enforcement is real, not theoretical: UL has litigated to consent decrees with seven-figure settlements, maintains public counterfeit notices, and counterfeit certification labels have driven product recalls. VDE runs a standing public register of unlawful users.

### The gate

**Raise this before building whenever the output could touch a shippable product, packaging, or advertising.** Concretely, stop and ask when the work is:

- a physical product or its packaging — **including a t-shirt**, which is itself a product placed on the market
- anything used to market or sell something
- a mockup that could be mistaken for production artwork

A poster, an editorial illustration, an album cover, or a UI is a much lower-risk context. Merch is the hard case, not the poster — a garment bearing a mark that misleads as to the form of CE is squarely within the prohibition.

## Inventing a mark

The reliable move, and it costs nothing aesthetically.

1. **Invent the acronym, and make it non-colliding.** Avoid CE, UKCA, UL, ETL, CSA, TÜV, VDE, ENEC, FCC, RCM, PSE, KC, CCC, GS, NOM, VCCI, BSMI, PSB — and avoid one-letter transforms of them, which is exactly what "substantially indistinguishable" catches.
2. **Break the geometry deliberately, in a direction the real rules forbid.** CSA's own list of prohibited alterations is a ready-made list of what reads as *not the real mark*: repositioned components, altered size relationships, condensed or expanded proportions, changed typeface, added graphic device. Doing one of those to *your own* invented mark is free; doing it to a real one is misuse.
3. **Give every fictional mark a fictional identifier.** This is the detail that sells it. Real marks always carry a body number, control number, file number, or licence number — UL's E-number, ENEC's CB number, Intertek's control number, Singapore's licence number. **A bare glyph is a tell.** An obviously fictional serial (`E00-0000`, `NB 0000`) reads as a prop to anyone who knows plates while matching the real visual grammar exactly.
4. **Never attach a real standard number to a fictional mark.** "Conforms to UL STD 62368-1" beside an invented monogram is precisely the confusion the certification bodies warn about.
5. **Never reproduce a tier-(b) mark at all** — not scaled, not recoloured, not traced.
6. **Build the family, not the mark.** Real plates carry marks from many bodies. One invented mark looks like a logo; four invented marks from four fictional bodies, each with its own geometry and identifier format, looks like a rating plate.

## How real marks actually sit on a plate

Verified by direct examination of real nameplates. This is the layout logic to reproduce — and it's the part that makes or breaks the illusion.

1. **Marks form a band or block, never interleaved with the data.** They're collected into one contiguous region — a horizontal band, a corner cluster, a right-hand field — and the electrical data occupies the rest.
2. **Optical centring, not baseline alignment.** Within a band, marks share an approximate vertical centre, but cap heights differ by **up to 3×**. Nothing sits on a common baseline. Nothing shares an x-height.
3. **Every mark brings its own typography** — serif, grotesque, condensed, stencil, mixed case. There is no type system across the band. **This is the single strongest authenticity signal.**
4. **Marks come with satellite text at unrelated sizes.** A file number at 40% of the monogram's height; a licence number as a row of individually boxed digits; a body number set as large as the mark itself. Satellite text does **not** align to the label's text grid.
5. **Roughly a third of "marks" are typographic, not pictorial** — a box containing `RoHS / 2011/65/EU`, `CAN ICES-3 (B)`, `CONFORMS TO UL STD…`, `ITE POWER SUPPLY`. Boxes of text read as marks.
6. **Gaps are irregular**, varying 2–3× within one band, because each mark carries its own mandated clear space and the assembler simply butts them together.
7. **Count by market.** Single-market product: 3–6 marks. Global IT product: 10–14. A world-SKU power adapter: 25+.
8. **Orientation is opportunistic** — a mark block may run 90° to the product's reading direction because that's where the space was.
9. **One or two marks anchor the block at large size** (usually the ones with the most internal text) **and the rest pack around them.**

**Why it looks this way, and why you must not "fix" it:** the CE regime requires one mark to stay visible and legible and forbids others from impairing it; each private mark's licence forbids altering its proportions; each scheme mandates its own adjacent identifier. **No authority anywhere can harmonise the set.** The label designer's only free variables are the position and overall scale of each intact lockup. Perfect optical alignment is therefore impossible in reality — which is why it reads as fake.
